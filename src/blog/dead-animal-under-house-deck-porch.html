Dead Animal Under the House, Deck, or Porch

I've written about a dead animal smell coming from inside a wall or attic, but under-structure spaces — a crawlspace, under a deck, under a porch — are a genuinely different problem, and I get calls for this specific scenario often enough that it deserves its own explanation.

Who's Usually Down There

Opossums, raccoons, and skunks are the animals I most commonly deal with in this exact spot. Washington's own wildlife agency describes opossums using "chimneys, attics, and spaces under houses, porches, and sheds" as den sites, and specifically notes they'll den nearly anywhere dry, sheltered, and safe. Skunks are called out separately — they frequently den under houses, porches, and sheds, and unlike an opossum simply moving into an existing gap, a skunk will actually dig under a foundation or concrete slab to make its own space.

Why This Is Genuinely Harder Than a Wall or Attic

An attic is at least a space you can stand or crouch in. A crawlspace or the gap under a deck is a different kind of access problem — low clearance, dirt, and often no clean path to wherever the animal actually ended up. Since a skunk may have dug its own tunnel rather than occupied one obvious cavity, the animal isn't necessarily sitting in a single accessible spot the way it might be in a wall — it could be well back from any opening you can actually see.

Why Skunks Specifically Change the Calculation

This is the real reason I'd rather you call for this one. Washington wildlife guidance describes a skunk's warning behavior before it sprays — foot-stomping, short charges — and confirms a skunk can spray five to eight times before it needs about a week to replenish. Multiple wildlife-control sources are direct about this: trapping a skunk safely takes real training and the right equipment, and startling one is what actually triggers spraying. A cornered skunk in a tight crawlspace is a worse version of a situation that's already better left to someone who does this regularly.

How This Actually Gets Handled

The real best-practice sequence, per Washington's own wildlife agency: identify the actual entry point first, confirm whether young are still present before doing anything permanent (sealing an entry with live animals still inside creates a much bigger problem than the one you started with), and only close things up once you're actually sure the space is clear.

Why It's Not Just a Smell Problem

Crawlspaces are already prone to excess moisture — the EPA's own guidance notes that damp crawlspace conditions are a common site for hidden mold growth, and that a bare-earth floor pulls moisture up through capillary action. A carcass decomposing in a space that's already moisture-prone compounds a problem that was already there, on top of the odor itself.

Preventing It From Happening Again

Washington's wildlife agency recommends closing potential entry points with quarter-inch hardware cloth, boards, or metal flashing, with all connections flush and secure — the same principle applies to deck and porch skirting, ideally with a buried footer at the base so a determined digger can't just go under it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What animal is most likely under my deck or porch? Opossums, raccoons, and skunks are the most common in this region — all three are known to den specifically in these under-structure spaces.

Is it more dangerous to remove an animal from under a deck than from a wall? Access is genuinely harder, and if it's a skunk, the confined space raises the real risk of spraying if it feels cornered — professional handling matters more here than in an open attic.

Should I seal the entry point myself once the smell is gone? Only after confirming no live animals, including young, are still inside — sealing too early is a well-documented way to make the problem much worse.

How do I keep this from happening again? Seal potential entry points with hardware cloth or flashing, and add a buried footer to deck or porch skirting so animals can't just dig underneath it.
