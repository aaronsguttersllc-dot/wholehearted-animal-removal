How to Get Rid of a Dead Horse: Your Real Options in Washington

If you're standing in a pasture right now trying to figure out how to get rid of a dead horse, here's a straight answer: there are really only four real options in Washington, and which one makes sense depends on your property, your budget, and how you feel about doing it yourself. Washington also requires disposal within 72 hours of death or discovery, so this isn't a decision to sit on. Here's an honest rundown of each option, with a link to the full detail on every one I've already written. If you're still working through the broader situation of an unexpected death — confirming what happened, deciding whether to call your vet first — my post on what to do first when your horse dies covers that whole process; this page is specifically about the disposal decision itself.

Option 1: Bury the Horse Yourself

This is legal in Washington, but only on properties that meet real, specific requirements — at least five acres, 300 feet from any well or water source, 300 feet from a neighbor's home, and three feet of soil cover, among other rules. Most residential properties don't actually qualify. I've written a full explanation of why the law is this strict, plus a breakdown of what it actually costs to do it right if your land does qualify.

Option 2: Hire the Burial Out

If your property qualifies but you'd rather not rent a backhoe and dig the hole yourself, hiring the burial out is a real middle option — you still keep your horse on the property, without the equipment logistics or physical toll of doing it alone right after a loss. See my full breakdown of what a hired burial involves if that fits.

Option 3: Private Cremation

Cremation is the right call if you want your horse's ashes returned rather than left on the property. Whole-horse cremation typically runs $600 to $2,000, with a real Washington provider's weight-based pricing running roughly $1.75 to $2.25 per pound. I don't offer cremation myself, but I'll tell you honestly if it's the better fit — see the full pricing breakdown here.

Option 4: Professional Removal

This is what I do — coming out, loading the horse, and transporting it, often coordinated directly with your vet's schedule the same day as a euthanasia. It's usually the simplest path when burial isn't legal on your land and cremation isn't what you want. I don't publish a flat rate since it depends on location and access, but see who actually handles dead animal pickup in Washington for where a service like mine fits in.

What About Rendering?

A lot of people assume a rendering plant will just come pick the horse up, and that's usually not the case — especially if the horse was euthanized. Most horses are put down with pentobarbital, which rendering doesn't break down, so renderers generally decline a euthanized carcass outright rather than risk it. I go into the full reasoning, including the real FDA history behind it, in what actually happens after a horse is euthanized.

How to Actually Decide

  • Does your property genuinely meet Washington's acreage and distance rules? If not, burial is off the table regardless of preference.
  • Do you want the ashes back? If yes, cremation is the only option that gives you that.
  • Can you physically or emotionally handle equipment rental and digging right now? If not, hiring the burial out or calling for removal makes more sense than DIY.
  • How fast do you need this resolved? The 72-hour window can rule out options that take longer to schedule.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you get rid of a dead horse in Washington? Burial (if your property qualifies), private cremation, or professional removal are the three realistic paths — rendering is rarely available, especially after euthanasia.

How do you dispose of a dead horse if you don't have acreage? Cremation or professional removal are the realistic options when your property doesn't meet Washington's burial requirements.

Can I just bury my horse on my own property? Only if your land meets real requirements under WAC 16-25-025 — minimum acreage, distance from water and neighbors, and proper depth. Most residential properties don't qualify.
