How to Dispose of a Deer Carcass in Washington

My post on what to do when you find a dead deer covers the first step — there's a Washington rule requiring you to call WDFW before you move wildlife found dead on your property, and almost nobody knows it exists. This post is the next question, and a different one: once you've made that call, what are your actual disposal options, which ones are legal, and what happens if you just haul it off somewhere and dump it?

The Call That Comes First

Briefly, since it's covered in depth elsewhere: under WAC 220-400-040, removing or disposing of wildlife found dead on your own property requires calling the Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife or Washington State Patrol communications first, before you move it. The remains technically belong to the state. It's a five-minute call, and everything below assumes you've made it — full detail here.

Option 1: Burial on Your Own Property

Washington's general dead animal disposal rule (WAC 246-203-121) is what governs this, and it applies to deer the same way it applies to a dog or a cat — it covers "dead animal" carcasses broadly, not just pets. The real requirements:

  • Disposal within 72 hours of death or discovery
  • At least three feet of soil cover over the carcass
  • At least 100 feet from any well, spring, or surface water
  • Not in a floodplain, and not anywhere it could reach groundwater

Worth noting how much more permissive this is than the livestock rule. A horse or cow falls under WAC 16-25-025, which demands five acres, 300-foot setbacks, and an annual cap on how much of your land you can use — requirements that rule out most properties entirely. A deer doesn't carry that burden. If you've got the space and a shovel, legal burial is genuinely available to most people here in a way it isn't for livestock.

Is It Illegal to Dump a Deer Carcass?

Yes — and I want to be precise about why, because this is the question people actually type into a search bar at 9pm with a deer in the back of a truck.

Dumping isn't a disposal method, legally speaking. WAC 246-203-121 requires disposal by a lawful method within 72 hours; hauling a carcass to a ditch, a logging road, a river, a vacant lot, or the back of someone else's acreage doesn't satisfy that, and it layers on Washington's solid waste and litter provisions on top. Add the WAC 220-400-040 wrinkle — the animal belongs to the state, not to you — and "I moved it off my property" is not the same thing as "I disposed of it."

Here's my honest limit on this one: I couldn't find a single statute that reads "it is illegal to dump a deer carcass" with a specific dollar penalty attached, and I'm not going to invent one or repeat a number I can't trace. What I can tell you is that there's no lawful path where dumping is the answer, and that the people most likely to notice are exactly the ones who'd report it. If the reason you're considering it is that burial isn't realistic on your property, that's a solvable problem — it's most of what I do.

Option 2: Landfill, and Why You Call First

Some transfer stations and landfills will accept animal carcasses and some won't, and the ones that do often want prior approval rather than a surprise in the back of a pickup. This mirrors what I've found on the household pet side, where approval from both the local health officer and the landfill operator is the real requirement rather than a default drop-off right. One phone call before you load the animal saves a wasted trip.

Does Chronic Wasting Disease Change Any of This?

It's a fair question now in a way it wasn't three years ago. Washington confirmed its first case of chronic wasting disease in July 2024 — a white-tailed deer found dead in the Fairwood area of north Spokane County, confirmed by WSU's Washington Animal Disease Diagnostic Laboratory. Four more cases have been confirmed since: three in Spokane County and one in Pend Oreille County.

The honest regional read: every confirmed Washington case so far is in the far northeast corner of the state, hundreds of miles from Pierce and Thurston County. That's the same honest framing I apply to rabies — the disease is real and worth understanding, and it is not currently a Pierce County problem.

Where it does matter for disposal: CWD is a prion disease, and prions are not destroyed the way bacteria and viruses are, which is why WDFW has issued rules restricting how carcasses move between areas. If you're in or hauling from an affected county, check WDFW's current transport and sampling requirements rather than assuming the general rule covers you — those rules have changed more than once since 2024. More on the disease itself in what actually kills deer around here.

Why the 72-Hour Clock Is Real

The deadline isn't bureaucratic padding. Noticeable odor typically starts within 24 to 72 hours, and a carcass left out draws scavengers — which is how a contained problem in one corner of a pasture becomes a scattered one across the whole property. If the deer is anywhere near a water source, that's a separate reason to move quickly; see what a carcass does to standing water.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you dispose of a deer carcass legally in Washington? Call WDFW or WSP first, then either bury it on your property (72 hours, three feet of cover, 100 feet from water, no floodplain) or arrange an approved landfill drop-off. Removal by a service is the third path.

Is it illegal to dump a deer carcass? Yes. Dumping doesn't meet WAC 246-203-121's requirement of lawful disposal within 72 hours, and the carcass legally belongs to the state rather than to you.

How deep do you have to bury a deer? At least three feet of soil cover under WAC 246-203-121 — notably less demanding than the livestock rule, which also requires five acres and 300-foot setbacks.

Can I just leave it to decompose naturally? Not within the 72-hour disposal requirement, and not without inviting scavengers and odor. Remote rangeland decomposition is a recognized method for livestock under specific distance rules, but it isn't a general permission slip.

Does chronic wasting disease affect how I dispose of a deer? In affected counties, yes — WDFW has carcass transport and sampling rules that have changed repeatedly since the state's first detection in July 2024. All confirmed Washington cases so far are in Spokane and Pend Oreille counties.
