Found a Dead Coyote? What to Know

Coyotes have adapted well to living near people — Washington's own wildlife agency describes them as present across nearly all of the state, including urban and suburban parks, greenbelts, and wooded spaces. Finding one dead, especially one that looks sick or hairless, raises a specific question worth answering honestly: what's actually going on with it.

The Most Likely Explanation: Mange, Not Rabies

If a coyote looked bald, scabby, or emaciated before it died, sarcoptic mange is by far the more likely explanation, not rabies. Mange is caused by mites that burrow into the skin, causing intense itching, hair loss, and thickened, crusted skin — a real, well-documented condition in coyote populations, including in Washington, per the state's own wildlife agency. As the disease advances, affected coyotes often become weak enough that they're seen wandering in daylight, which is exactly the kind of sighting that gets people worried about rabies when the actual cause is a skin condition, not a neurological one.

Why This Region Makes Rabies Especially Unlikely

I've already established on this site that bats are Washington's only confirmed rabies reservoir. That holds for coyotes specifically, too — Washington's DOH states plainly that rabid coyotes, like rabid raccoons, foxes, and skunks, "have not been identified recently in Washington," even though the virus can theoretically spill over from a bat. Mange, by contrast, is a documented, ongoing condition in local coyote populations. If you're trying to make sense of a sickly-looking coyote in Pierce or Thurston County, the odds favor mange by a wide margin.

Is Mange a Risk to My Dogs?

Yes, and this is worth taking seriously. Sarcoptic mange mites can spread to dogs through direct contact, and the mites can survive for a period in an environment a mangy coyote used regularly, like a den or resting spot, even without direct nose-to-nose contact. Washington's wildlife agency itself acknowledges this cross-species risk to domestic dogs. If a mangy coyote has been denning or resting near your property, keeping dogs away from that specific area is a real, practical precaution.

What Washington Law Says

Washington law requires notifying WDFW or the Washington State Patrol by phone before removing wildlife found dead on your property, and coyotes are wildlife under the state's own broad definition. I couldn't confirm how actively this specific rule gets enforced for a coyote versus a larger game animal, but it's the law as written. Washington's general dead animal disposal rule also appears to apply once you're clear to remove it — burial at least three feet deep, at least 100 feet from any well or water source, handled within 72 hours.

What to Actually Do

  • A hairless, scabby-looking coyote is almost certainly mange, not rabies — Washington has no confirmed rabies reservoir in coyotes
  • Keep dogs away from any area a mangy coyote has been denning or resting in, since mange can spread to pets
  • Call WDFW or WSP before removing it, since Washington law requires notification before removing wildlife found dead on your property
  • Never feed coyotes or leave pet food or garbage accessible — WDFW is direct that this is what pulls coyotes into closer, riskier contact with people in the first place

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a hairless, sickly coyote mean rabies? Almost certainly not — sarcoptic mange, a mite-caused skin condition, is a well-documented, common cause of that appearance in coyotes, including in Washington. Rabies has never been confirmed in Washington coyotes.

Can my dog catch mange from a dead coyote? Yes, this is a real, documented risk — mange mites can spread through direct contact or from an area the coyote used, like a den site, not just from the animal itself.

Are coyotes common in Pierce and Thurston County? Washington's wildlife agency describes coyotes as present across nearly all of the state, including suburban and urban areas, so yes.

Do I need to call anyone before removing a dead coyote? Washington law technically requires notifying WDFW or WSP before removing any wildlife found dead on your property, coyotes included.
