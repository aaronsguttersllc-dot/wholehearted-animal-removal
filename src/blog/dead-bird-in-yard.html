Dead Bird in Your Yard: What to Do

A dead bird in the yard seems like the simplest possible version of this problem — until you learn there's a real federal law that technically applies to wild birds and doesn't apply to almost anything else I write about on this site. Here's what it actually means, and what's genuinely fine to do in practice.

The Law Most People Have Never Heard Of

The Migratory Bird Treaty Act makes it illegal to possess a protected wild bird, or any part of one — including feathers, nests, or eggs — without a permit. That's not limited to killing a bird; the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service is explicit that this extends to picking up feathers or remains you find, with no built-in exception just because the bird died naturally or you didn't cause it. As of the end of 2024, USFWS actually created a real, no-permit "salvage" allowance specifically for this situation: the public can pick up a dead migratory bird, its feathers, or an inactive nest, but whatever you pick up has to be donated to a permitted party or destroyed within 7 days. Keeping it isn't allowed.

Which Birds This Actually Applies To

The Act only covers native migratory species. A handful of common backyard birds are specifically excluded because they're introduced, non-native species: European starlings, house sparrows, and rock pigeons. If what you're dealing with is one of those, none of the federal handling rules above apply at all.

What's Actually Fine to Do With a Single Bird

Washington's own wildlife agency addresses this directly: for a single dead bird, their real guidance is to double-bag it, use a shovel rather than bare hands, and put it in the trash — or bury or incinerate it as alternatives. In practice, normal disposal is fine for one bird found dead of no obvious cause. The federal salvage rule above legally covers this exact situation as long as you're not keeping the remains.

What About Bird Flu?

This is a real, current concern, not outdated caution. The CDC's actual guidance is to avoid direct, bare-hand contact with a dead wild bird — use disposable gloves or an inverted bag if you need to handle it, double-bag it for disposal, and wash your hands afterward. The CDC is also clear that the current public health risk from bird flu to the general public is low, even though the virus is circulating in wild bird populations. This isn't a reason to panic over one dead bird in the yard — it's just a reason to use gloves rather than bare hands, which is honestly good practice regardless.

When It's Worth Reporting

A single dead bird generally isn't something you need to report anywhere. Washington's wildlife agency's real stated threshold for prioritizing lab testing is significantly higher — multiple birds, somewhere in the range of 15 to 25 or more found sick or dead in the same area over a short period. That's the kind of pattern that points to a real outbreak concern, not one bird that died of natural causes in your yard.

The Disposal Rule Itself

Once you've handled the federal question, Washington's general dead-animal disposal rule (WAC 246-203-121) applies to a bird the same as any other small animal — no bird-specific exception exists. That means disposal within 72 hours, and if you're burying it, at least three feet of soil cover and at least 100 feet from any well, spring, or surface water. If the bird ended up stuck somewhere less straightforward — a chimney, for instance — that's a different situation with its own real safety concerns.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it illegal to touch or move a dead bird? Technically, federal law regulates possessing a protected wild bird's remains, but a real no-permit exception covers picking one up as long as you dispose of or donate it within 7 days rather than keeping it.

Can I just throw a dead bird in the trash? For a single bird, yes — Washington's wildlife agency's own guidance recommends double-bagging and disposing of it in the trash, or burial or incineration as alternatives.

Should I worry about bird flu from one dead bird? The CDC says current public risk is low, but it's still smart to avoid bare-hand contact — gloves or an inverted bag, then wash your hands.
