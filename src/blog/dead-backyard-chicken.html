What to Do When a Backyard Chicken Dies

I've written about dead livestock removal and mentioned that Washington's legal definition of livestock is broader than most people expect — it actually includes poultry. That surprises a lot of backyard chicken owners, so this post is about what that really means in practice for a single bird, which is a genuinely different situation than a dead cow or horse.

Yes, Chickens Are Legally Livestock in Washington

Washington's carcass disposal rule (WAC 16-25-025) defines "livestock" to include poultry, waterfowl, and rabbits, not just cattle, horses, and goats. On paper, that means the same rule that governs disposing of a dead cow technically governs a dead chicken too — including a requirement that most properties cannot legally bury livestock at all unless they're at least five acres.

The Real Exception That Actually Matters Here

This is the detail that makes the rule practical for a backyard flock: Washington's own regulation carves out an explicit exception for burying "a single carcass weighing less than two hundred pounds," regardless of property size. A chicken obviously qualifies. I want to be precise about one thing I couldn't fully confirm: whether the rule's other setback requirements — generally 300 feet from water sources and 50 feet from property lines for livestock burial — still apply to that small-carcass exception, or whether they're waived along with the acreage minimum. Since I couldn't confirm that either way, the safer approach is to treat those distances as if they still apply when you're deciding where to bury a chicken.

Which Rule Actually Governs a Chicken That Died Suddenly

Here's a real legal nuance worth knowing: Washington law presumes that livestock found dead from an unknown cause died of disease, which technically routes it to the stricter WSDA-administered rule (WAC 16-25-025) rather than the more general, more permissive rule that covers pets. In practice, since almost every backyard chicken death is discovered after the fact rather than witnessed, the livestock rule — softened by the small-carcass exception above — is the one that actually applies most of the time.

Composting Is a Real, Legitimate Option

Composting is explicitly recognized as an approved disposal method under Washington's carcass rules, and it's genuinely well-suited to a backyard flock, since it reuses materials many keepers already have on hand — wood shavings, straw, or sawdust from the coop. The general method: layer a carbon source like straw or wood chips with the bird in the center of a bin, keeping at least six inches of material around the carcass on all sides. Done correctly, soft tissue breaks down in about a week.

If Multiple Birds Die at Once: A Real, Different Situation

One dead chicken from old age, predation, or a known injury is a different situation than multiple birds dying suddenly or showing unusual illness. Washington's Department of Agriculture maintains a Sick Bird Hotline specifically for this — if more than one bird dies unexpectedly or your flock shows unusual illness, that's worth a call rather than assuming it's routine. Washington has had real, confirmed avian influenza detections in backyard flocks as recently as spring 2026, concentrated seasonally around fall and spring wild-bird migration — worth knowing about without treating it as a constant, year-round alarm. If you handle a sick or dead bird during a suspected outbreak, Washington's Department of Health recommends gloves and an N95 mask if contact is unavoidable, plus thorough handwashing afterward.

Real, Common Causes of a Single Chicken Death

  • Predation — hawks, raccoons, foxes, and dogs are common causes, often sudden
  • Egg binding — a hen unable to pass an egg, which can become genuinely life-threatening
  • Heat stress, respiratory illness, or simply old age
  • None of these require professional removal on their own — basic gloves and handwashing are a reasonable precaution for handling a single bird yourself

When This Is Actually a DIY Task vs. When to Call

Honestly, a single backyard chicken is usually a manageable DIY situation — it's legally simple to compost or bury under the small-carcass exception, and basic gloves and hand hygiene cover the real health precautions. Professional help makes more sense if you don't have safe burial or composting space, if multiple birds are affected at once, or if you'd simply rather not handle it yourself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it actually legal to bury a chicken in my backyard? Yes — Washington's livestock disposal rule includes a specific exception for a single carcass under 200 pounds, which covers a chicken regardless of property size.

Can I compost a dead chicken? Yes, composting is an explicitly approved disposal method under Washington's carcass rules, and it's a practical option for a backyard flock.

What if more than one chicken dies at once? That's different from a single death — contact Washington's Sick Bird Hotline rather than assuming it's routine, especially during spring or fall migration season.

Do I need professional help for one dead chicken? Usually not — it's a legally simple, low-risk task for a single bird. Professional removal makes more sense for multiple deaths or if you lack space to bury or compost.
