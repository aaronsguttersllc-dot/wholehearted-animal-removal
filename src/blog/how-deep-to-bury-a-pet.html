How Deep to Bury a Pet in Washington

I've covered the full legal picture for burying a dog or cat in Washington already, and it mentions the three-foot depth requirement in passing. This is the deeper look at that one number — where it comes from, why it's genuinely not arbitrary, and what actually goes wrong when it's ignored.

The Actual Rule

Washington's general dead animal disposal code (WAC 246-203-121) requires at least three feet of soil cover over a buried pet, on top of the distance-from-water and timing requirements covered in the main post. Three feet is the minimum — covering deeper is always allowed and, depending on your soil and what's nearby, can be the safer call.

How Washington's Depth Compares to Other States

Three feet sits at or above what's typical nationally. Pennsylvania's general guidance calls for roughly two feet of cover, for comparison, while some states set deeper minimums specifically tied to their local water table depth. Washington's requirement isn't an outlier or an unusually strict number — it's a solid, middle-to-upper baseline compared to how other states approach the same problem.

Why the Depth Actually Matters

This isn't a rule for its own sake. Proper depth does two real things: it physically deters scavenging animals from digging up and disturbing the remains, and it helps contain odor that would otherwise attract them in the first place. A shallow burial doesn't just risk looking bad if it's disturbed — it creates a real, documented pathway for a scavenging animal to be exposed to whatever was in the deceased pet's system.

The Real Risk: Secondary Poisoning

If a pet was euthanized, its remains contain pentobarbital, the drug used for the procedure. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service has documented real, confirmed cases of wildlife — including bald eagles — dying from secondary pentobarbital poisoning after scavenging on inadequately buried or improperly disposed-of euthanized animals. This isn't a hypothetical risk raised to scare people into compliance; it's a documented cause of death in protected wildlife, which is exactly the kind of harm the depth-and-cover requirement is designed to prevent.

Practical Considerations Beyond the Minimum

  • Dense clay or rocky soil can make reaching three feet genuinely difficult by hand — know your ground before you commit to a spot
  • A location too close to fence lines, septic systems, or areas you might landscape or dig again later can create problems down the road, even if the depth itself is compliant
  • If your pet was euthanized, the pentobarbital risk is a real reason to go beyond the three-foot minimum if your soil and situation allow it
  • Heavy rain and soil settling over time can reduce effective cover, so packing the soil well and considering a slightly deeper burial gives more of a real margin

When Digging Deep Enough Isn't Realistic

Not every yard, soil type, or physical situation makes a proper three-foot burial practical, especially on short notice after a loss. That's a completely legitimate reason to consider cremation or professional removal instead of forcing a burial that can't actually meet the depth requirement.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is three feet really the legal minimum in Washington? Yes, under WAC 246-203-121 for pets and most non-livestock animals. Deeper is always allowed and sometimes advisable depending on your soil and situation.

What actually happens if a pet is buried too shallow? It creates a real risk of scavenging by wildlife, which can expose them to whatever was in the pet's system — including pentobarbital from euthanasia, a documented cause of secondary poisoning in wildlife like bald eagles.

Should I bury deeper than three feet if my pet was euthanized? It's a reasonable extra precaution given the documented secondary-poisoning risk, if your soil and physical situation allow it.

What if I can't dig three feet deep myself? That's a legitimate reason to consider cremation or professional removal instead — not every yard or soil type makes a compliant burial realistic.
