Found a Dead Raccoon in Your Yard? What to Know

I've written before about how to tell if a dead animal has rabies and established that bats are Washington's only confirmed rabies reservoir. Raccoons are worth their own explanation, because the national picture and the Washington picture genuinely diverge here — and because rabies isn't actually the most likely explanation for a raccoon found dead in daylight.

Raccoons and Rabies: A Real Regional Difference

In much of the eastern United States, raccoons are a genuine, major rabies reservoir — a raccoon-specific strain of the virus spread through the mid-Atlantic and Northeast after a 1977 translocation of infected raccoons, and the CDC notes that within that zone, 10% of raccoons that expose people or pets test positive. That's a real national fact, but it's not Washington's story. Washington's own Department of Health is direct: bats are the only known rabies carrier in this state, and raccoon-variant rabies has never been reported here — the last confirmed rabid wild carnivore of any kind in Washington was a coyote in 1930.

So Why Do Raccoons Die in Daylight Around Here?

Canine distemper virus is the real, common answer. Washington's own wildlife agency states plainly that distemper is a significant contributor to raccoon mortality in this state. It's a genuinely serious illness — mucous discharge from the eyes and nose, coughing, stumbling, disorientation, seizures, and a loss of the normal wariness around people, including raccoons wandering or collapsing in daylight even though they're typically nocturnal. I'll be honest that distemper's symptoms can look alarming enough to be mistaken for rabies by an untrained eye — the two genuinely cannot be told apart with certainty without lab testing of brain tissue, but given how rare rabies actually is in Washington raccoons, distemper or another illness is the far more likely explanation here.

The Real Risk: Raccoon Roundworm

I've written about raccoon roundworm before in the context of a dead animal in a pool — eggs shed in raccoon feces survive normal chlorine levels entirely. The same organism matters here too: raccoons return to consistent latrine sites, and a dead raccoon on your property is often near or at a spot it used repeatedly while alive. The CDC's real precautions for cleaning up a raccoon latrine apply to that surrounding area — gloves, an N95 respirator if you're in a confined space, and boiling water on contaminated hard surfaces. I want to be precise here: I couldn't find a source confirming the carcass itself sheds roundworm eggs, so the real risk is the area around it, not necessarily the animal itself.

What Washington Law Requires

Washington law requires notifying WDFW or the Washington State Patrol by phone before removing wildlife found dead on your property, and the statutory definition of "wildlife" is broad enough to include raccoons. I couldn't confirm how actively this gets enforced for an everyday raccoon versus a larger game animal, but it's the rule as written. Once you're clear to remove it, the general dead animal disposal rule applies — burial at least three feet deep, at least 100 feet from any well or water source, handled within 72 hours.

When to Actually Be Concerned

  • Multiple dead raccoons in the same area over a short period points toward a distemper die-off, which wildlife agencies describe as a regularly occurring event, not a rabies signature
  • A single raccoon acting aggressively or unprovoked is more consistent with rabies than one that appears sick, disoriented, or stumbling
  • Either way, don't handle a live raccoon showing neurological symptoms — leave that to wildlife control or animal control
  • Report unusual sightings or multiple deaths to WDFW rather than trying to diagnose it yourself

Safe Handling

  • Never handle a dead raccoon bare-handed
  • Wear gloves and avoid contact with any bodily fluids
  • Wash your hands thoroughly afterward
  • Keep pets and kids away from the carcass and the surrounding area until it's removed

Frequently Asked Questions

Do raccoons carry rabies in Washington? Raccoon-variant rabies has never been documented in Washington. Bats are the state's only confirmed rabies reservoir — raccoons are a much bigger rabies concern in the eastern U.S. than here.

Why would a raccoon die in daylight if it's not rabies? Canine distemper virus is a real, common cause of raccoon deaths in Washington, and its neurological symptoms can look similar to rabies without lab testing to confirm which one it actually is.

Is it safe to be near where a dead raccoon was found? Raccoon roundworm eggs can persist in an area a raccoon used regularly. Basic precautions — gloves, hand-washing, avoiding direct contact — are the real, sourced safety measure.

Do I need to call anyone before removing a dead raccoon? Washington law technically requires notifying WDFW or WSP before removing any wildlife found dead on your property, raccoons included.
