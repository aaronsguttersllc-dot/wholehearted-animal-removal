Found a Dead Bear? What Washington Law Requires

Everything else I've written about is small to medium wildlife. A dead bear is a genuinely different situation — both because black bears are real, documented residents of this part of Washington, and because state law treats a dead bear with more scrutiny than most other wildlife.

Yes, Bears Are Genuinely Here

Pierce and Thurston County fall within a WDFW hunting district with documented black bear activity, including game management units bordering Mount Rainier National Park itself. This isn't theoretical — 2025 news reporting documented real bear sightings and captures within Pierce County specifically, including Lakewood, along I-5, and near Puyallup. Washington's statewide black bear population is estimated at roughly 20,000 to 22,000 animals. Grizzly bears are a different story entirely — they're confirmed absent from this region, with Washington's only real grizzly presence limited to the Selkirk Mountains in the far northeast corner of the state.

Why a Dead Bear Gets More Scrutiny

Washington law requires notifying WDFW or the Washington State Patrol before removing any wildlife found dead on your property, the same baseline rule that applies to smaller wildlife. But bears specifically sit inside WDFW's dangerous-wildlife incident-tracking system — as of September 2025, WDFW began publicly posting all black bear incident reports on the same dashboard used for cougar, wolf, and grizzly reports. Real, documented enforcement activity backs this up: Washington Fish and Wildlife Police investigate illegal bear killings as a matter of course, including a real, recent felony wildlife- trafficking conviction involving bear gallbladders and paws sold on the black market.

Natural Death, Vehicle Strike, or Something Illegal?

This distinction matters more for a bear than it typically does for smaller wildlife. WDFW's own research tracking GPS-collared bears found that poaching and wounding loss together account for roughly a fifth of documented bear mortality in one major study population — a real, meaningful share, not a rare edge case. Washington maintains a real "Turn In A Poacher" program with a 24-hour tip line and rewards up to $500 for information leading to a conviction. I want to be honest that the underlying mortality data I found is from a North Cascades research population, not Pierce or Thurston County specifically — I'd treat it as the best available regional context, not a locally precise number.

What Washington Law Requires

  • Call WDFW or WSP before removing a dead bear from your property — the same notification rule that applies to other wildlife, taken more seriously here given the dangerous-wildlife tracking system
  • Don't approach or handle it yourself — general wildlife-safety guidance is consistent that you shouldn't approach any large animal, even one that appears dead, until it's been confirmed safe
  • If you suspect the bear was killed illegally, Washington's TIP line (877-933-9847) is the real channel for that, separate from routine wildlife reporting
  • Unlike a deer or elk, there's no legal salvage permit process that lets you keep a bear found dead — it stays state property regardless

Who to Actually Call

For an emergency or immediate danger, call 911. For a routine report, WDFW's regional office covering Pierce and Thurston County (Region 6, Coastal) can be reached at 360-902-2936, or by emailing WILDCOMM@dfw.wa.gov. For a suspected poaching case specifically, the TIP line above is the right channel.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are black bears actually a real concern in Pierce and Thurston County? Yes — documented sightings, hunting district data, and a genuine statewide population in the tens of thousands confirm real presence in this region, including areas near Mount Rainier.

Are grizzly bears a concern here? No — grizzlies are confirmed absent from this part of Washington, limited to the Selkirk Mountains in the far northeast.

Can I keep a dead bear I find, like a roadkill deer? No — unlike deer and elk, there's no legal salvage permit for bears. It remains state property regardless of how it died.

What if I think a bear was poached? Report it through Washington's TIP line (877-933-9847) — a real program with real rewards for information leading to a conviction.
