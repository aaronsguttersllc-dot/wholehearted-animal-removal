How Horse Mortality Insurance Actually Works

I've written about what to do first when a horse dies and about what a necropsy actually involves. This post covers something that catches a lot of insured horse owners off guard at the worst possible moment: mortality insurance has real requirements that can genuinely conflict with how fast you need to move under Washington law.

The Core Conflict, Stated Plainly

Washington requires disposal of a deceased horse within 72 hours of death or discovery. Most horse mortality insurance policies require the owner to get authorization from the insurer before disposing of the body, and nearly all carriers require a necropsy to process a mortality claim. Those two requirements are pulling in opposite directions on a clock that's already short, and it's a genuine, documented tension — not a rare edge case.

Why Insurers Require Authorization First

A mortality claim is effectively an insurer verifying that a real loss happened the way it was reported, and the body is the primary evidence. Disposing of a horse before the insurer has had a chance to arrange or approve a necropsy can jeopardize the claim entirely — from the insurer's side, once the body is gone, so is the ability to independently confirm cause of death. This is standard practice across the industry, not a quirk of one company's policy.

What This Means in a Real Emergency

  • If your horse is insured, call your insurer or agent as close to immediately as possible after death — before making any disposal arrangements
  • Ask directly whether they require a necropsy and how quickly they can authorize one
  • Keep your policy documents and your insurer's after-hours contact information somewhere you can actually find them in a crisis, not just in an email from years ago
  • If you can't reach your insurer within a timeframe that respects Washington's 72-hour rule, document the situation and your attempts to reach them as thoroughly as you can

Necropsy and Insurance Go Together

A necropsy, performed through WADDL at WSU, is generally what an insurer is actually asking for when they require documentation of cause of death. Coordinating the necropsy and the insurance claim as one connected process, rather than two separate tasks, is the realistic way to move fast enough on both fronts at once.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to call my insurance company before burying or cremating my horse? If the horse is insured, yes — most mortality policies require authorization before disposal, and disposing of the body first can jeopardize your claim.

Does horse mortality insurance always require a necropsy? Essentially all carriers require one to process a mortality claim, which is a real reason to coordinate the necropsy and the insurance call together rather than treating them as separate steps.

What if I can't reach my insurer within Washington's 72-hour disposal window? Document your attempts to reach them as thoroughly as you can. This is a genuine, documented conflict in the industry — not something you're doing wrong by running into it.
