Found a Dead Duck? What to Know

I've written about dead birds generally and about a dead fish in a pond. A dead duck sits in between those two posts in a real way — it's covered by the same federal bird law, but it's also a genuine signal about what's happening in the water it was found near, which is worth its own explanation.

Yes, the Same Federal Law Applies

Ducks and geese are fully protected migratory birds under the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, same as the songbirds I've already covered — the law doesn't discriminate between live and dead birds, or between species. As of a rule that took effect at the end of 2024, the general public can now legally pick up and possess any migratory bird found dead without a federal permit, waterfowl included, as long as you didn't cause the death, you don't keep it for personal use, and you either donate it to a permitted institution or dispose of it within 7 days.

One Dead Duck Can Mean More Are at Risk

This is the real reason a dead duck deserves more attention than a single songbird might. Avian botulism is a well-documented, real cause of waterfowl die-offs — a bacteria that produces a paralyzing neurotoxin, thriving in warm, stagnant, low-oxygen water, most common in late summer and early fall. Federal wildlife health researchers describe a genuine, self-perpetuating cycle: maggots feeding on a dead bird concentrate the toxin, and other birds or scavengers that eat those maggots get poisoned in turn. The blunt, real conclusion from that research: a single carcass left in a wetland can actually prolong an outbreak. That's a real, practical reason removing a dead duck promptly matters beyond just the smell.

Ducks and Bird Flu: A Different Role Than Backyard Chickens

I've written separately about what avian flu means for a backyard flock. Wild ducks play a genuinely different role in that story — the CDC is direct that wild waterfowl are natural reservoirs for bird flu viruses, and ducks specifically can carry and spread certain strains without showing any signs of illness themselves. Washington's own wildlife agency has confirmed avian flu in wild birds here, including waterfowl, since 2022. A dead duck isn't automatically a bird flu case, but it's part of why the same general precautions apply.

What Washington Law Says

Washington law requires notifying WDFW or the Washington State Patrol before removing wildlife found dead on your property, and ducks and geese qualify as wildlife under the state's own broad definition — the rule doesn't carve out an exception for birds. I'll be honest that I couldn't find a clear answer on whether this rule works differently for a bird found on a fully public waterway versus your own private pond; the rule's language is framed around your own property, so a private pond is clearly covered, but a public shoreline is less clearly addressed. When in doubt, a call is the safer move either way.

What to Actually Do

  • If it's a single duck with no other signs of illness nearby, standard precautions apply — gloves, no bare-hand contact, thorough handwashing afterward
  • If you find multiple dead waterfowl in or near the same pond or wetland, treat that as a real signal, not a coincidence, and report it to WDFW
  • Call WDFW or WSP before removing a dead duck from your property, since Washington law requires notification first
  • Keep pets away from the water and the carcass until it's been dealt with

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it legal to pick up a dead duck I found? Yes, under a rule that took effect at the end of 2024, the general public can legally pick up and dispose of or donate a migratory bird found dead, without a federal permit — but Washington's own notification rule still applies first.

Why does one dead duck matter more than one dead songbird? Waterfowl die-offs from avian botulism can spread through a whole wetland, and a single leftover carcass can actually prolong the outbreak by feeding the cycle that poisons more birds.

Do wild ducks carry bird flu? Wild waterfowl, ducks especially, are natural reservoirs for bird flu viruses and can carry and spread certain strains without appearing sick themselves.

Do I need to call anyone before removing a dead duck? Washington law technically requires notifying WDFW or WSP before removing any wildlife found dead on your property.
