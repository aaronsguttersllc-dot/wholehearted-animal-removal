Found a Dead Hawk or Owl? What to Know

I've already covered dead birds generally — the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, the federal salvage rule, and basic handling precautions. Hawks and owls are technically covered by that same law, but there's a real, distinct reason raptors deserve their own explanation, and it's not one most people have heard of.

Yes, Raptors Are Federally Protected — Just Not the Way You'd Guess

Hawks and owls are protected under the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, same as the songbirds and other wild birds I've already written about. What's genuinely interesting: raptors weren't part of the original 1918 law at all — it was written for insectivorous and game birds. Hawks, owls, and eagles were only added in a 1972 amendment. Before that, raptors were legally treated as vermin in a lot of places and shot without consequence. Worth being precise about one more distinction: the separate Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act applies only to eagles specifically, not to hawks or owls, which rely on the Migratory Bird Treaty Act alone for their federal protection.

The Real, Documented Cause Worth Knowing: Rodenticide Poisoning

This is a genuinely important, well-documented issue. When a raptor eats a rodent that's already ingested rat or mouse poison, the poison passes to the raptor too — a real phenomenon called secondary anticoagulant rodenticide poisoning. A peer-reviewed review found exposure in a wide range of raptor species, from roughly 10 percent of barn owls tested up to 100 percent of red-tailed hawks at one facility, though the same research is honest that population-level harm isn't yet proven, only individual-level harm. Locally, a Washington-specific project run by the Urban Raptor Conservancy with PAWS Wildlife Center found rodenticide exposure in the large majority of raptors they tested from multiple species. If you use rodent poison on your property, this is a real reason to consider snap-trap alternatives instead.

Can You Tell if That's What Killed It?

Not reliably just by looking. Some signs — unusual bleeding from the mouth or nose, bruising, blood in droppings — can be associated with rodenticide poisoning, but researchers studying this only confirm it through necropsy and toxicology testing, specifically checking liver tissue for poison residue alongside physical signs of internal bleeding. A dead raptor with no visible injury genuinely could be a poisoning case, a window strike, a territorial fight with another raptor, disease, or something else entirely — visual inspection alone can't sort that out.

Why You Might Actually Want to Report It, Not Just Dispose of It

Washington's wildlife agency maintains an official tool for reporting sick, injured, or dead wildlife, specifically to help monitor disease and other conditions affecting wildlife health — this becomes especially relevant during avian flu outbreaks, since raptors including bald eagles have been affected. The Urban Raptor Conservancy's Washington rodenticide research project also accepts reports of dead raptors as part of their ongoing study. Neither of these expects you to personally transport a dead raptor anywhere — reporting what you found and where is the useful part.

Real Handling Precautions Beyond the Usual "Don't Touch It Bare-Handed"

Hawks and owls have sharp talons and hooked beaks capable of real puncture injuries — a genuinely different hazard than handling a songbird. Wildlife rehabilitation centers consistently recommend thick leather gloves and fully covering the bird before any handling. Most of that specific guidance is written for live, injured raptors that can still grip or strike defensively, but the sharpness of talons and beak remains a real cut hazard during handling either way.

What to Actually Do

  • Don't handle a dead hawk or owl bare-handed — thick gloves are the real standard, not just disposable ones
  • If you use rodenticide on your property, know that it's a real, documented cause of raptor deaths through secondary poisoning
  • Consider reporting a dead raptor to WDFW, especially if you notice unusual bleeding or if multiple dead birds turn up in the same area
  • Keep kids and pets away from the bird until it's handled

Frequently Asked Questions

What actually kills wild hawks and owls? Secondary rodenticide poisoning from eating already-poisoned rodents is a real, well-documented cause, alongside collisions, territorial fights, and disease.
