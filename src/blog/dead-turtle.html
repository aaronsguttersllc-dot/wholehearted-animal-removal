Found a Dead Turtle? What to Know

A dead turtle isn't something I get asked about often, but there's a real, genuinely interesting story behind which species you're actually likely to find in Pierce or Thurston County, and one of them is rarer and more legally significant than most people would guess.

What You're Actually Most Likely to Find

The red-eared slider — the common pet-store turtle with the red stripe behind its eye — is not native to Washington and shows up in local ponds almost entirely because someone released a pet. Washington's wildlife agency is direct that releasing a non-native species into state waters is against the law. I want to be precise here, though: WDFW's own materials note that successful reproduction of sliders in the wild hasn't actually been documented in Washington, and the species may not be well suited to this climate — so while individual released pets do turn up, including in real, documented die-offs at spots like Wapato Lake in Pierce County, it's more an ongoing release problem than a self-sustaining invasive population in the way that phrase usually implies.

The Rarer, More Significant Find: The Western Pond Turtle

This is a genuinely real, state-endangered species, listed in Washington since 1993. It historically ranged from Snohomish south through Thurston County before disappearing from Puget lowlands by the 1980s. What makes this directly relevant to this area: WDFW reintroduced a captive-bred population to a managed wetland site in Pierce County in 1996, and that recovery population still exists today, one of only two in South Puget Sound. Statewide numbers have grown from under 200 turtles in the 1990s to roughly 800 today, across six managed populations. If you find a dead turtle anywhere near a known recovery wetland in Pierce County, there's a real chance it's this species, not a released pet.

Why the Distinction Actually Matters Legally

Washington's wildlife statute defines "wildlife" broadly enough to include reptiles, and the general rule requiring notification before removing wildlife found dead applies the same way to turtles as anything else. But a state-endangered species like the Western pond turtle carries a real, additional layer: Washington law separately makes it a crime to possess an endangered species without authorization, dead or alive. I couldn't find a source spelling out a turtle-specific removal procedure beyond that general framework, so I'll say plainly: report any dead turtle before moving it, and treat one found near a known recovery site with real extra caution rather than assuming it's just an escaped pet.

A Real, Documented Threat: Shell Disease

This is a genuine, current conservation concern specific to this species. A WDFW survey found signs of ulcerative shell disease in as many as half of the Western pond turtles examined, linked to a newly identified fungus. It's a real factor working against the same recovery population this area is home to.

Dead or Just Dormant?

I want to be honest that I couldn't find strong Washington- specific or wildlife-agency guidance on this exact question — what I found comes from general reptile-care sources, not a wildlife authority. With that caveat: native turtles here brumate submerged in mud or underwater during colder months, not lying exposed on land, so a motionless turtle found in the open — especially outside the colder months — is more likely genuinely dead than dormant. If you're not sure, the safer move is not to handle it yourself.

What to Actually Do

  • If you're near a pond or lake known for turtle releases, a red-eared slider is the more likely find
  • If you're near a managed wetland in Pierce County, consider that it could be the real, protected Western pond turtle
  • Report a dead turtle to WDFW before removing it, same as any other wildlife found dead on your property
  • If you're not confident it's actually dead rather than dormant, don't handle it — wait or call for guidance instead

Frequently Asked Questions

Are turtles in Washington ponds usually native or pets? Most commonly encountered turtles are red-eared sliders, a non-native species present almost entirely because of pet releases, which are illegal under state law.

Is there a real native, protected turtle in this area? Yes — the Western pond turtle is state-endangered, with a real recovery population reintroduced to a managed Pierce County wetland in 1996.

Do I need to report a dead turtle before removing it? Yes — Washington's general wildlife notification rule applies to turtles the same as other wildlife, and possessing a dead endangered species carries additional legal weight.

Could a motionless turtle actually be alive? Native turtles brumate submerged in mud during cold months, not exposed on land — so one found still and exposed, especially outside winter, is more likely dead than dormant, though I'd treat this as general guidance rather than confirmed wildlife-agency advice.
