How to Tell If a Dead Animal Has Rabies

This is a real, honest question I get, usually from someone who found a dead animal that acted strangely before it died, or who just wants to know if it's safe to deal with. I'd rather give you the real answer than a falsely reassuring one: there are genuine warning signs, but you often can't actually be certain just by looking.

Real Warning Signs, Before Death

The CDC lists real, documented signs: excessive drooling or foaming at the mouth, aggression or unprovoked biting, unusual tameness in a wild animal that should be avoiding people, general disorientation or stumbling, and in later stages, paralysis — often starting in the hind legs. CDC is direct about an important caveat, though: not every rabid animal shows aggression or drooling. A normal-looking animal can be infected, and an animal with none of the classic signs isn't automatically safe.

Here's the Honest Limit

You cannot reliably tell whether a dead animal had rabies just by looking at the body. This isn't me being overly cautious — it's the actual international veterinary standard. Confirming rabies requires laboratory testing of fresh brain tissue; visual inspection alone can neither confirm nor rule it out, even when an animal showed textbook symptoms beforehand. In Washington, that testing happens at the state lab in Shoreline.

What's Actually Common in Washington

This is genuinely useful context, and it should make you less worried, not more: Washington's Department of Health is direct that bats are the only known rabies reservoir in this state. Other animals can occasionally get infected from a bat, but they're not an independent source the way bats are, and that's a real difference from many other parts of the country where skunks, raccoons, foxes, or coyotes carry it more broadly. Looking at this year's actual confirmed cases statewide: 18 positive cases through August, every one of them a bat, with cases concentrated in King and Lewis counties and none reported in Pierce or Thurston. Rabies is real, but it's genuinely rare here, and it's specifically tied to bats — I go into what that actually means for a real bat encounter in a separate post on dead bats specifically.

If You or a Pet Actually Had Contact

This is the one part of this topic that's a real medical decision, not a DIY one. If there's been a bite, scratch, or contact between saliva and broken skin or your eyes, nose, or mouth, wash the area immediately with soap and warm water for a full ten minutes, then contact a healthcare provider and your local health department right away. They're the ones who actually assess exposure risk and decide whether post-exposure treatment is needed — that's not something to guess at on your own, and it's not something to wait on.

Safe Handling, Regardless

  • Never handle a dead wild animal bare-handed
  • Use heavy gloves — leather or doubled rubber/nitrile work
  • Avoid contact with saliva or any exposed nervous tissue
  • Double-bag the remains and seal them securely
  • If a bat is involved and testing might be needed, keep it refrigerated rather than frozen, and contact public health before disposal
