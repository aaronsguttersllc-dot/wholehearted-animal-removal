Dead Animal in Your Chimney: What to Do

A bird looking for a nesting spot, or a raccoon or squirrel looking for a den, can end up stuck in an uncapped chimney and never make it back out. This is a real, specific scenario I get calls for, and it's worth more than just a smell fix — there's a genuine safety angle most people don't realize.

Why Animals End Up in There

Birds sometimes fall in during flight or while investigating a chimney as a nesting spot. Raccoons and squirrels treat an uncapped flue as a real den option. In both cases, the smooth or narrow walls of a chimney flue make climbing back out genuinely difficult, and an animal that can't escape eventually dies where it got stuck.

This Is a Real Fire and Carbon Monoxide Risk

This is the part that makes this more than a smell problem. A carcass or accumulated nesting material blocking a flue can force toxic combustion byproducts — including carbon monoxide — back into your living space instead of safely up and out, if the fireplace is used with the blockage still in place. The Chimney Safety Institute of America specifically lists animal or bird nests obstructing a flue as a real, documented cause of this kind of blockage. On top of that, dry nesting material — twigs, feathers, fur — is genuinely combustible, and a single ember can ignite it.

Why the Smell Gets Worse When You Use the Fireplace

A carcass stuck partway down a flue produces the smell you'd expect from decomposition anywhere, but it's often most noticeable specifically when the fireplace is in use — heat and draft pull the odor down into the room rather than letting it sit quietly unnoticed the way it might with the fireplace cold and unused.

Who Actually Handles This

This is genuinely a two-skill-set job in a lot of cases. A chimney sweep can provide structural access — removing the damper, working from the smoke shelf, or accessing from the roof with a light and camera to actually locate the animal. Handling the animal itself, especially a raccoon, is more often wildlife control's role, since raccoons can carry rabies, distemper, and roundworm, and require real precautions and, in a lot of places, a specific wildlife handling license. Don't assume one call covers both parts.

What Not to Do

  • Don't reach into the flue yourself, especially blind, without knowing what's actually in there
  • Don't light a fire to try to "smoke it out" — this can kill the animal, ignite nesting material, and push smoke and carbon monoxide into your home instead of solving anything
  • Don't assume it's safe to use the fireplace again until the blockage is actually confirmed clear

Preventing It From Happening Again

A proper chimney cap with a spark arrestor is the real, standard fix — it's also actually required by building code for chimneys serving solid-fuel appliances, with specific mesh-opening size requirements. A cap does double duty: keeping animals out and reducing spark-related fire risk at the same time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a dead animal in my chimney dangerous, or just unpleasant? Genuinely dangerous, not just unpleasant — a blocked flue can force carbon monoxide back into your home if the fireplace is used, and dry nesting material is a real fire risk.

Should I call a chimney sweep or a wildlife control company? Often both — a sweep provides structural access, while actually handling the animal, especially a raccoon, is more commonly wildlife control's job.

Can I just light a fire to clear it out myself? No — this is a well-documented bad idea that can ignite nesting material and push smoke and carbon monoxide into your living space.

How do I stop this from happening again? A proper chimney cap with a spark arrestor keeps animals out and is standard, code-referenced practice.
