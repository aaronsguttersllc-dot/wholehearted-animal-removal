Why Is It Illegal to Bury a Horse in Washington?

I hear this question phrased like burying a horse is flatly against the law, full stop — and that's not quite accurate. It's legal on a genuinely small number of properties that meet every requirement. What trips people up isn't a blanket ban, it's how strict and specific the real requirements are, and what actually happens if you skip them. I've written before about what those requirements are and what it costs to meet them — this post is about the "why," and it goes further than either of those.

It's Not Automatically Illegal — the Requirements Just Rule Out Almost Everyone

Washington's rule for burying livestock (WAC 16-25-025) sets real, specific conditions: at least five acres, a burial site 300 feet from any well, spring, or surface water, 300 feet from a neighbor's residence, 50 feet from a property line that isn't yours, at least three feet of soil cover completed within 24 hours of burial, and no burying in a flood-prone area or 100-year floodplain. There's also a cap most people don't realize exists: you can only use 10 percent of your property, or one acre — whichever is greater — for this kind of burial in a given year. A single site is limited to one animal over 1,000 pounds. Meet every one of those conditions, and burial is legal. Miss even one, and it isn't — there's no partial credit.

The Real Reason the Law Exists

Most explanations of this rule stop at groundwater contamination — and that risk is real; peer-reviewed research on carcass burial confirms pathogens like Salmonella and E. coli can move through soil via leachate as a carcass decomposes. But there's a more direct reason baked into Washington law that explains why the rule doesn't bend for individual cases: under RCW 16.36.102, any livestock found dead of an unknown cause is legally presumed to have died of disease, unless proven otherwise. The disposal rule isn't written for the average, uncomplicated death — it's written assuming the worst case, every time, because the law doesn't get to assume otherwise. That's also why the rule applies the same way to every species of livestock, not just horses.

What Actually Happens If You Bury One Illegally

This is the part I haven't seen explained honestly elsewhere. Washington law (RCW 16.36.110) makes violating this chapter — which includes the carcass disposal rule — a gross misdemeanor, and each day the violation continues counts as a separate offense. The director of the Washington State Department of Agriculture also has the authority to bring an action in superior court to stop an ongoing violation. In practice, enforcement is typically complaint-driven — a neighbor reporting smell or runoff, or a local health officer getting involved — rather than routine inspection of private property. But "typically not caught" isn't the same as "not actually illegal," and a documented gross misdemeanor is a real legal exposure, not a technicality.

The Rare Property That Actually Qualifies

In practice, this usually means real acreage well beyond a typical residential lot, land with no well, spring, creek, or pond anywhere near the required distances, no neighboring home within 300 feet, and ground that isn't in a floodplain. It's not impossible — some working farms and larger rural parcels genuinely qualify — but it rules out the overwhelming majority of properties I get calls about, which is exactly why most people end up looking at other options.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it illegal to bury a horse anywhere in Washington? Not everywhere — it's legal only on properties that meet every requirement of WAC 16-25-025: minimum acreage, distance from water and neighbors, proper depth and cover, and staying within the annual land-use cap. Most residential properties don't qualify.

What's the actual penalty for illegal burial? Violating Washington's animal health chapter, which includes the carcass disposal rule, is a gross misdemeanor under RCW 16.36.110, with each day of an ongoing violation counted separately.

Why does the law presume a dead animal had a disease? Under RCW 16.36.102, livestock found dead of an unknown cause is legally presumed to have died of disease unless proven otherwise — which is why the disposal rule is strict by default rather than case-by-case.
