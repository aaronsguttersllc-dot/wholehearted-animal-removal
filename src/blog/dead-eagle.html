Found a Dead Eagle? What Washington Law Requires

I mentioned in the dead hawks and owls post that eagles fall under a separate, stricter federal law than other raptors — the Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act. That law is genuinely stricter in a way worth explaining on its own, especially since eagles are a real, common presence in this part of Washington.

What the Law Actually Prohibits

The Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act makes it illegal to possess a bald or golden eagle, alive or dead, or any part of one — including feathers — without a federal permit. The law draws no distinction based on how you came to have it: a feather you find on the ground is legally treated the same as one taken directly from the bird. A first offense carries a real penalty of up to a $100,000 fine and up to a year in prison, per the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's own published figures; a second offense can run up to $250,000 and two years.

Where a Dead Eagle Actually Goes

There's a real federal facility for this — the National Eagle Repository, operated by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in Colorado, which collects and stores eagle remains and distributes them to enrolled members of federally recognized Tribes for religious and cultural use. As a practical matter, though, a Washington resident who finds a dead eagle shouldn't try to ship it to Colorado directly — the real first step is reporting it to Washington's own wildlife agency, which coordinates from there.

The Real Native American Exception

This is a genuine, formal federal accommodation, not an informal workaround. Enrolled members of federally recognized Tribes can apply for eagle remains from the Repository for religious and cultural purposes — ceremonies including healing, marriage, and naming — through an official permit application process that requires tribal enrollment certification. Approved applicants receive a lifetime permit, and requests are filled in the order received from whatever the Repository currently has in stock, since it's often genuinely backlogged.

Are Eagles Actually Common Here?

Yes, genuinely. Washington's bald eagle population collapsed to just 105 known breeding pairs by 1980 before recovering roughly 10 percent a year over the following decades — eagles were removed from the state's own endangered and sensitive species list in 2016, and Washington's wildlife agency now describes the population as robust. As of the most recent county-level data I could find, Pierce County had 51 known eagle territories and Thurston County had 17, concentrated along the marine shoreline, rivers, and lakes this area genuinely has a lot of. That data is from 2010, so the real current number is likely similar or higher given the broader recovery trend, but I want to be upfront that I couldn't confirm a more recent county-level count.

What Actually Kills Eagles Around Here

Nationally, trauma, electrocution, and lead poisoning are the three leading documented causes of eagle death, with lead poisoning specifically responsible for close to a third of bald eagle deaths in one federal study. A real, regional study tracking eagles at Washington State University's raptor rehabilitation program over 18 years found toxic blood lead levels in roughly half of bald eagles tested from this region, with the majority of those cases showing up right after deer and elk hunting season — a real, documented pattern tied to eagles scavenging on gut piles left behind. Separately, real, recent local reporting documented bald eagles found dead in Snohomish County with lethal levels of rat poison in their systems, tied to secondary rodenticide exposure — the same real issue I've already covered for raptors generally.

What to Actually Do

  • Don't touch, move, or keep any part of the bird, including a single feather — that's the law, not just a suggestion
  • Photograph it where it is if it's safe to do so, and note the exact location
  • Call Washington Fish and Wildlife to report it rather than handling it yourself
  • If you use rodenticide on your property, know that it's a real, documented cause of eagle deaths in this region specifically

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it illegal to pick up a single eagle feather? Yes — the law makes no exception for how you came to possess it, whether taken from the bird or found on the ground.

What's the actual penalty for possessing eagle parts without a permit? Up to a $100,000 fine and a year in prison for a first offense, per U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service figures, with harsher penalties for repeat violations.

Are bald eagles really common in Pierce and Thurston County? Yes — both counties have documented eagle territories concentrated along the shoreline, rivers, and lakes, and the statewide population has recovered substantially since the 1980s.

Can Native American tribal members legally possess eagle feathers? Yes, through a real, formal federal permit process tied to tribal enrollment, allowing possession of remains from the National Eagle Repository for religious and cultural use.
