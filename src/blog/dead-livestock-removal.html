Dead Livestock Removal: What to Do When a Cow, Llama, Goat, or Other Farm Animal Dies

A lot of what I've written so far talks about horses specifically, since that's what most people searching end up asking about. But horses aren't the only animals I handle, and they're not the only ones Washington's disposal law covers. If you've lost a cow, llama, goat, sheep, or other farm animal, the same rules — and mostly the same real options — apply. Here's the honest breakdown for the rest of the herd.

What Counts as "Livestock" Under Washington Law

Washington's animal health statute (RCW 16.36.005) defines "livestock" as horses, mules, donkeys, cattle, bison, sheep, goats, swine, rabbits, llamas, alpacas, ratites, poultry, waterfowl, and game birds. That's a broad list, and it matters because the same carcass-disposal rule I write about for horses (WAC 16-25-025) doesn't only apply to horses — it applies to every animal on that list. If you own cattle, llamas, goats, or sheep, you're working under the exact same law, not a lighter or different one.

The Same Burial Rules Apply, Regardless of Species

Washington's routine livestock carcass disposal rule (WAC 16-25-025) doesn't set different standards by species — it's one rule for the whole livestock category. On-property burial requires at least five acres (except for a single carcass under 200 pounds), a burial site at least 300 feet from any well, spring, or surface water, 300 feet from a neighbor's residence, and 50 feet from a property line not your own. The carcass has to sit at least three feet below the surface, fully covered within 24 hours of being placed in the ground, and the site can't be in a flood-prone area or within a 100-year floodplain. A single burial site is capped at 1,000 pounds of carcasses, or one animal over 1,000 pounds — which matters more for cattle than it might for a goat or sheep, since a mature cow or bull can individually exceed that cap. There's also an annual limit on how much of your property you can use this way: 10 percent of the property or one acre, whichever is greater.

In other words, owning several acres doesn't automatically mean you're set up to bury a cow, a llama, or several goats over time — the same acreage, distance, and annual-use math applies no matter which animal you're dealing with.

Real Alternatives Beyond Burial

The Washington State Department of Agriculture recognizes several disposal methods beyond burial, each with its own real requirements — this is the part that gets skipped in most horse-focused content, including mine, so it's worth laying out here:

  • Composting — a legitimate, WSDA-recognized option for livestock mortality, but it has to comply with Washington's solid waste rules (chapter 70.95 RCW and WAC 173-350), not just be a pile in a back field
  • Incineration — requires an approved incineration facility or a mobile air curtain incinerator, permitted under chapter 70.94 RCW
  • Landfill — requires prior approval from both your local health officer and the landfill operator; it's not a default drop-off option
  • Rendering — legally, only a rendering plant licensed under chapter 16.68 RCW can accept a carcass; availability has shrunk statewide since the 1990s, and if the animal was euthanized, most renderers will decline it outright over drug-residue concerns
  • Natural decomposition on rangeland — permitted in some cases, but only at least 1,320 feet from wells, water, and roads, out of public view, and with the landowner's permission

None of these are simple do-it-yourself options in most residential or small-acreage situations — they exist for larger operations with the land, permits, or equipment to use them correctly.

Why the Rules Don't Bend by Species

It surprises people that a goat or a llama is governed by the same strict rule as a 1,200-pound horse. But Washington law (RCW 16.36.102) actually presumes any livestock found dead from an unknown cause died of disease, unless proven otherwise — and that presumption is exactly why the disposal rule doesn't carve out exceptions by size or species. The law is written around the worst-case scenario, not the average one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do the same burial rules apply to cattle, goats, and llamas as they do to horses? Yes — Washington's carcass disposal rule (WAC 16-25-025) applies to livestock broadly, which is legally defined to include cattle, sheep, goats, llamas, alpacas, and more, not just horses.

Can I compost a dead farm animal instead of burying it? It's a recognized option, but it has to follow Washington's solid waste composting rules (chapter 70.95 RCW and WAC 173-350) — it isn't as simple as an open pile.

Will a rendering plant take a dead cow or goat? Only if it's a licensed rendering plant under chapter 16.68 RCW, and availability has dropped significantly statewide. If the animal was euthanized, most renderers will decline it regardless of species.
