Who Picks Up Dead Animals? Your Real Options in Washington

If you've just found a dead animal and you're not sure who you're even supposed to call, you're not alone — this is one of those situations nobody explains until you're standing there needing an answer. The real answer depends a lot on where the animal is and what kind of animal it is, so here's an honest breakdown of who actually handles what in Washington, and where a service like mine actually fits in.

If It's On a Public Road

Roadkill on a county road is generally the county's responsibility, not yours — Pierce County Public Works and similar county departments handle dead animals in county rights-of-way. On state highways, WSDOT maintenance crews do the physical removal, but the usual way to report one is through the Washington State Patrol or local law enforcement, who relay it to WSDOT. Either way, if it's genuinely in the road or the road shoulder, a call to your county's public works line or the non-emergency police line is the right first move — not a private removal service.

If It's a Small Pet That Died at Home

Your vet almost never comes to your house for this — even for a pet that dies at home rather than in-clinic, vets typically arrange cremation through a third-party pet crematory rather than sending someone to collect the body. In King County, animals under 15 pounds can legally go in your regular household garbage, and larger animals can be taken to a vet, animal shelter, pet cemetery, or Cedar Hills Regional Landfill with an approved waste clearance (206-477-4466). Lewis County's Animal Shelter accepts drop-offs for a small fee, though they don't offer pickup. You can also legally bury a small pet on your own property in most cases — Washington's general dead-animal-disposal rule doesn't require the acreage a horse or other livestock burial does, just real setbacks from wells and water. I go into that distinction in detail in a separate post on disposing of a dog or cat.

If It's a Horse or Other Livestock

This is where things get genuinely more complicated, and it's also where I spend most of my time. Animal control's actual scope, at least in King County, is "dangerous animals" and "loose livestock on public roadways" — not routine carcass removal on private property. A dead horse or cow on your own land is legally your responsibility to resolve, and Washington's rules for legally burying livestock are genuinely strict: a minimum of five acres, real distance requirements from wells, water, and neighboring homes, and a cap on how much of your property you can use for burial in a given year. I've written a full breakdown of those rules and what your real options are if you don't qualify, plus a separate look at what it actually costs if your property does. Rendering is rarely available, especially if the animal was euthanized, which leaves most people choosing between legal burial, private cremation, or professional removal.

If It's Wildlife

For dead wildlife causing a public safety concern, Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife is the right contact, not county animal control. For a dead deer or other wild animal that's simply on your own property and not a safety issue, it's generally on you to deal with it — animal control typically won't respond to that call. One genuinely interesting side note: if a driver hits a deer or elk on the road and wants to keep it, WDFW actually requires a free roadkill salvage permit within 24 hours. Not relevant to most people calling me, but a real example of how specific Washington's wildlife rules actually get.

Why People Call a Private Removal Service

  • The property doesn't have the acreage to legally bury a large animal
  • It needs to be handled the same day, not whenever a county office gets to it
  • The animal is large livestock in a spot that's genuinely hard to access or move
  • There's no vehicle suited for transporting a large or heavy animal
  • It's simply too much to handle alone, physically or emotionally, right after a loss

None of the free or low-cost public options are wrong to try first — I'd rather you know all of your real options than assume you need to hire someone. But when none of them actually fit your situation, that's exactly when a call like mine makes sense.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who do I call for a dead animal in the road? Your county's public works department for a county road, or local law enforcement/WSP for a state highway — not a private removal service.

Will animal control pick up a dead pet from my house? Generally no. Animal control's typical scope is dangerous or loose live animals, not routine dead-pet pickup on private property.

Can I just bury a dead animal on my own property? It depends heavily on the animal. A small pet has much simpler rules than a horse or other livestock, which faces real acreage and distance requirements under Washington law.

What if I don't have anywhere else to turn? That's exactly the situation a private removal service exists for — reach out and I'll tell you honestly what your real options are.
