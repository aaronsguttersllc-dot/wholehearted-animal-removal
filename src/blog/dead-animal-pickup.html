Dead Animal Pickup: What Actually Happens When You Hire It Out

I've already written about who to call depending on your situation — county roads, wildlife, pets at home, livestock — and some of those free or low-cost options are genuinely worth trying first. This post is for the point after that decision's already made: you've decided a pickup service is the right call, and you want to know what actually happens once you make it.

What I Actually Pick Up

Pickup isn't limited to one type of animal. I handle large livestock — horses, cattle, llamas, goats, sheep — household pets like dogs, cats, rabbits, birds, and other small pets, and wildlife cases like deer, raccoons, and opossums on request. The process below is largely the same regardless of size, though a 1,000-pound horse and a house cat obviously need different equipment on the truck.

What I'll Ask You Before Pickup

  • A phone number I can actually reach you at
  • The address where the animal is located
  • How accessible the animal is — a field, a barn, a backyard — since weight and access together determine what equipment and vehicle I bring
  • What kind of animal it is, and roughly its size — small, medium, or large works fine
  • How long the animal has been deceased, since that affects timing

None of this is paperwork for its own sake — every one of those answers changes what I show up with, which is exactly why a real conversation gets you a more accurate answer than a flat online price ever could.

Scheduling, Including Around a Vet

Pickup can be scheduled in advance or handled the same day, depending on the situation. When an animal hasn't been euthanized yet, I coordinate directly with your veterinarian's timing rather than making you manage two separate schedules — especially in emergency situations, where ongoing communication in the moment matters most. If I can't be on-site at the exact same time as the vet, your situation stays a priority and I get there as quickly as I genuinely can.

Where Pickup Actually Falls Short of a Free Option (And When It's Worth It Anyway)

I'd rather be straight about this than oversell it: a county public works line, animal control, or a landfill drop-off can be cheaper or even free in the right situation, and I've laid those out honestly elsewhere on this site. Pickup earns its cost when those free options genuinely don't fit — no acreage to legally bury a large animal, the animal's too large or awkwardly placed to move without equipment, it needs handling the same day rather than whenever an office gets to it, or it's simply too much to manage alone, physically or emotionally, right after a loss.

What Happens After Pickup

Where the animal goes next depends on the animal and the situation. Rendering is rarely available, especially for a euthanized animal, since most renderers decline a carcass over drug-residue concerns. For horses and other livestock, disposal generally means burial, cremation, or continued transport handled the right way, not left to chance. Statewide and out-of-state transport is available when a situation calls for it — I've coordinated necropsy transport to university veterinary programs, including delivering a horse to the University of Oregon.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do you pick up any type of animal, or just horses? Large livestock, household pets, and wildlife cases on request — the intake process is the same regardless of animal type.

How fast can pickup happen? Same-day is often realistic depending on location and access; advance scheduling is always welcome and coordinated closely with your vet when timing matters.

What information do you need before you can give me a quote? A phone number, the address, how accessible the animal is, what kind of animal and roughly its size, and how long it's been deceased.

Is pickup always the right call? Not necessarily — some free county options are worth trying first. I'd rather tell you that honestly than assume you need to hire me.
