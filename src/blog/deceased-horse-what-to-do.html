Deceased Horse: What to Do First When Your Horse Dies

I've written in detail about what happens after a planned euthanasia, but a lot of horses die a different way — colic, a fall, old age, or just found down in the pasture one morning with no warning at all. If that's where you are right now, here's what to actually do first, before getting into burial, cremation, or removal.

Confirm What Happened Before You Do Anything Else

If the death wasn't witnessed or expected, it's worth calling your vet even though the horse has already died. A vet exam, or a necropsy if you want a real answer, is the only way to actually determine cause of death rather than guess — and that matters most exactly in the cases where nobody saw it coming. A necropsy is especially worth doing if there are other horses on the property, if you suspect illness or toxic exposure, or if you just want to know rather than always wonder. Until then, it's worth covering the animal and avoiding unnecessary handling, especially bare-handed, if the cause isn't clear.

A Real Washington Rule Most Owners Don't Know About

Washington maintains an official list of reportable animal diseases (WAC 16-70-020), and it includes a genuinely broad catch-all category: an "unexplained increase in dead or diseased animals." Licensed veterinarians and labs are legally required to report conditions on that list to the State Veterinarian. In practical terms, this means that if something is genuinely wrong — not just one horse that colicked, but something that looks like it could be disease-related — getting a vet involved isn't just good practice, it connects you to the person who actually carries the legal responsibility to flag it if it matters.

Common Reasons a Horse Dies Suddenly

Just for context, since it's a natural question in the moment: colic, traumatic injury, and cardiac events are among the most commonly documented causes of sudden equine death. Cardiac causes alone account for roughly half of sudden deaths studied in exercising horses, though sudden death isn't exclusively a cardiac event. None of this replaces an actual diagnosis — it's just useful to know that sudden, unexplained death happens for real, documented reasons, not because something was necessarily missed beforehand.

What Not to Do

  • Don't handle the animal extensively, especially bare-handed, if the cause of death isn't clear
  • Don't attempt to bury the horse without confirming your property actually meets Washington's real legal requirements first
  • Don't wait too long to decide — Washington requires disposal within 72 hours of death or discovery, which limits your options the longer you wait
  • Don't assume rendering is available — it rarely accepts a carcass after euthanasia, and isn't a given even outside that situation

It's Okay to Not Decide Instantly

Losing a horse unexpectedly is a real shock, and the 72-hour window, while real, doesn't mean you need to have everything figured out in the first hour. Take the time to actually confirm what happened and talk to your vet before locking in a decision about disposal — a clearer head now usually means a decision you won't second-guess later.

Frequently Asked Questions

My horse died unexpectedly — what's the first thing I should do? Confirm the death, avoid unnecessary handling if the cause isn't clear, and call your vet — even though the horse has already died, a vet exam or necropsy is the real way to determine cause.

Do I have to report my horse's death to anyone? Not directly in most cases, but if disease is suspected, your veterinarian carries a legal reporting duty under Washington's reportable disease rules — another real reason to get one involved.

How much time do I have to decide what to do? Washington requires disposal within 72 hours of death or discovery, which is real but still gives you room to think clearly rather than panic.
