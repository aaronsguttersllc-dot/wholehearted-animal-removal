Dead Animal in Your Pool: What to Do

This happens more than people expect, especially on properties near open space or water. A raccoon, opossum, or bird finds its way into a pool and can't get back out. Here's what's actually involved once that's happened — it's more than a skimmer job.

Why This Happens

Wild animals usually end up in a pool trying to get a drink, or while fleeing a predator, and once they're in, they're often stuck — a pool's smooth, vertical walls give them nothing to climb, unlike the sloped edge of a pond or stream they'd naturally use to get out. Panic sets in, and drowning can happen within 10 to 15 minutes.

Why This Is a Real Contamination Concern

A decomposing animal releases real bacteria into the water — E. coli and Salmonella among them — along with parasites, including Cryptosporidium, which is notably resistant to chlorine. This isn't just an unpleasant thing to look at; it's an actual water-safety issue, and the risk is higher for kids, older adults, and anyone with a weaker immune system.

One Specific Risk: Raccoons

If the animal was a raccoon, there's a genuinely important detail most people don't know: raccoon roundworm eggs are not killed by chlorine or salt at any normal pool concentration. Heat is what actually destroys them, not pool chemicals. That doesn't mean the pool can't be made safe — it means physical removal, filter service, and a real decontamination protocol matter more than just running your normal chemical routine.

What to Actually Do

  • Wear gloves and use a net or skimmer to remove the animal — avoid direct contact
  • Don't just resume swimming once it's out — the water still needs treatment
  • Shock/super-chlorinate the pool rather than relying on your normal daily chlorine level
  • Check and service the filter and pump, since contamination doesn't stop at the water's surface
  • Wait until chlorine levels return to a normal safe range before swimming again

Why Normal Chlorine Levels Aren't Enough

Your pool's everyday chlorine level is meant for routine maintenance, not for a contamination event like this. The real standard used for a solid-waste contamination incident calls for actively raising free chlorine to 2 ppm and holding it there for at least 30 minutes — a genuinely different, more aggressive step than your normal passive level. This is treated seriously enough in public pool codes that it's a distinct, defined protocol, not a "just add a little extra" situation.

Preventing This From Happening Again

A real, genuinely effective product exists for this: a floating escape ramp (sold under names like FrogLog) that lets small animals climb out along the pool wall before they exhaust themselves. Pool covers and fencing also reduce how often wildlife ends up in the water in the first place.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it safe to swim after removing a dead animal from my pool? Not immediately — the water needs an actual shock/super-chlorination treatment, not just skimming the animal out, before it's genuinely safe again.

Is a raccoon in the pool a bigger concern than other animals? Yes — raccoon roundworm eggs survive normal pool chlorine and salt levels entirely. Heat, not chemicals, is what actually destroys them.

How do I keep animals from falling into my pool? A floating escape ramp lets smaller animals climb out before they drown, and a pool cover or fencing reduces how often they end up in the water at all.

How common is this? There's no real tracked data on how often this happens — it's a genuine, if not exhaustively documented, occurrence on properties near open space.
