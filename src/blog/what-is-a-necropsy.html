What Is a Necropsy?

I mention necropsies in my post on what to do when a horse dies unexpectedly, and it's a term a lot of horse owners have heard but never actually had explained. Here's what it actually is, what it can tell you, and what it costs in Washington.

The Basic Definition

A necropsy is the animal equivalent of a human autopsy — a post-mortem exam done to determine cause of death or identify disease or injury. There's a real distinction worth knowing between two versions of it. A field necropsy is an abbreviated exam an attending vet can do on-site, focused on the chest and abdominal cavities to catch the most common causes of death — it's faster and leaves the body more intact. A full laboratory necropsy is the complete, systematic version, done at a diagnostic lab, and can include histopathology (examining tissue under a microscope) along with bacterial, viral, and toxicology testing.

What It Can Actually Tell You

A necropsy can identify cause of death, reveal disease processes that weren't obvious beforehand, catch adverse drug reactions, and — importantly if you have other horses — confirm or rule out a contagious condition that could put the rest of your herd at risk. It can also uncover something unrelated, like a chronic issue that had been quietly present all along.

What It Actually Costs in Washington

Washington State University runs the state's real diagnostic lab for this — WADDL, the Washington Animal Disease Diagnostic Laboratory in Pullman. Their own published pricing lists an equine necropsy at $250, plus a $10 accession fee on all submissions. Add-ons exist too: a spinal cord evaluation runs an extra $60, and there's a $300 surcharge specifically for cases involving pending or potential litigation. Histopathology, often necessary to actually pin down a cause of death, is billed separately. That's real, published in-state pricing — not a national estimate that may not reflect what you'd actually pay here.

How the Logistics Actually Work

WADDL needs a fresh, chilled cadaver, submitted as quickly as possible — their own guidance states plainly that diagnostic quality deteriorates the longer the delay. If a few days' delay is genuinely unavoidable, freezing is accepted but reduces how much the exam can reliably tell you. In practice, the realistic path for a Washington horse owner is through your regular vet — they either perform a field necropsy themselves or coordinate getting the horse or samples to WADDL, and WADDL asks that submitters call or email them directly for those logistics rather than guessing at the process.

How Soon It Needs to Happen

Decomposition starts immediately after death, and diagnostic value drops as time passes. The real, honest guidance here is "as soon as possible" rather than a precise cutoff — same-day is ideal, and WADDL's own instructions emphasize getting a chilled cadaver to them quickly rather than citing an exact hour count. This is part of why deciding relatively quickly after an unexpected death matters, even while you're still processing what happened.

When It's Actually Worth Doing

  • Multiple horses are sick, or the rest of your herd could genuinely be at risk
  • The death was sudden and unexplained, with no clear cause
  • You suspect toxin exposure or poisoning
  • You need documentation for an insurance claim
  • The horse had a prolonged or costly treatment and you want to know whether it was the right call

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a horse necropsy cost in Washington? WADDL's own published pricing lists $250 for an equine necropsy plus a $10 accession fee, with add-ons like a $60 spinal cord evaluation and separate histopathology billing.

How soon after death does a necropsy need to happen? As soon as possible — ideally the same day. Decomposition affects diagnostic quality, and WADDL emphasizes quick, chilled submission over a specific hour-based cutoff.

Do I need to transport my horse to WSU myself? Your regular vet is the realistic starting point — they can do a field exam themselves or help coordinate getting the horse or samples to WADDL.

Is a necropsy always necessary? No — it's most valuable when the cause of death isn't clear, other animals could be at risk, or you specifically want documentation or answers rather than moving straight to disposal.
