Dead Deer Removal: What to Do If You Find One on Your Property

A dead deer shows up in more yards and pastures around here than people expect, and most folks have no idea what they're actually supposed to do about it. There's a real Washington rule involved that almost nobody knows exists, so here's an honest breakdown of what the law actually says, what the real health risks are, and where a removal service like mine fits in.

If You Hit a Deer on the Road

If it's genuinely in the road or on the shoulder, it's usually not something you need to handle yourself. On a county road, that's your county public works department's job. On a state highway, report it to the Washington State Patrol or local law enforcement, who notify WSDOT maintenance crews to remove it. WSDOT isn't dealing with this rarely, either — the department removed 39,258 wildlife carcasses from state highways between 2019 and 2023, and deer alone accounted for roughly 54 percent of that total, about 21,265 animals. Most get buried at designated disposal sites, though a few areas with especially heavy roadkill volume send carcasses to a permitted composting facility instead.

If a Deer Died on Your Own Property (The Rule Most People Don't Know)

This is the part that surprises almost everyone I talk to. Under Washington law (WAC 220-400-040), if you want to remove or dispose of wildlife found dead on your own property, you're required to call the Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife or Washington State Patrol communications first — before you move it — and give them your name, address, phone number, and a description and location of the animal. The remains technically belong to the state, and the rule only covers disposal, not keeping the meat. It's a five-minute phone call, but it's a real legal requirement, not a suggestion, and it's the kind of detail that never shows up in generic "how to deal with a dead animal" articles.

Once you've made that call, Washington's general dead animal disposal rule (WAC 246-203-121) governs how and when you actually dispose of it: within 72 hours of death or discovery, and if you're burying it, at least three feet of soil cover, at least 100 feet from any well, spring, or surface water, and not in a floodplain or anywhere it could reach groundwater. It's the same rule I write about for dogs and cats — deer fall under it too, since it applies broadly to "dead animal" carcasses, not just pets.

If You Want to Keep the Meat (Roadkill Salvage)

This mostly applies to a deer or elk you've just hit with a vehicle, not one you found already dead — but it's worth knowing either way. Anyone who takes possession of a vehicle-killed deer or elk carcass needs a free, printable roadkill salvage permit from WDFW within 24 hours, and the entire carcass, including entrails, has to be removed from the road right-of-way. As of a 2025 rule change, hunters in Clark, Cowlitz, and Wahkiakum counties also have to follow additional chronic wasting disease sampling requirements — not the counties I typically serve, but a real example of how specific these rules have gotten.

The Real Health Reasons Not to Let It Sit

A dead deer isn't just unpleasant to look at — it's a real safety consideration, especially if you have pets or kids on the property. Noticeable odor typically starts within 24 to 72 hours, and depending on the season, a carcass can take anywhere from about 18 to over 100 days to fully decompose. In the meantime, it draws scavengers — coyotes, raccoons, and birds of prey — and dogs that get to it can pick up parasites or pathogens from direct contact. Washington confirmed its first cases of chronic wasting disease in wild deer in 2024, in Spokane and Pend Oreille counties — that's Eastern Washington, well outside the area I serve, but it's a real, developing concern for the state and part of why wildlife officials take carcass handling seriously enough to require that phone call in the first place.

When It Makes Sense to Call a Removal Service

  • The deer is large, in an awkward spot, or somewhere you don't want to dig
  • You have dogs or kids on the property and want it handled quickly, not within the full 72-hour window
  • You'd rather not make the WDFW notification call and handle the carcass yourself
  • It's been sitting long enough that it's no longer a simple job

None of this requires hiring anyone — plenty of people make the call to WDFW, bury it properly themselves, and never need me. But when you'd rather not deal with it directly, that's exactly the kind of call I take.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to call anyone before removing a dead deer from my property? Yes — Washington law (WAC 220-400-040) requires you to notify WDFW or Washington State Patrol communications by phone before removing wildlife found dead on your property, even if you're just planning to bury it.

Can I just bury a dead deer in my yard? In most cases, yes, once you've made the required notification call — as long as the burial site has at least three feet of cover and is at least 100 feet from any well, spring, or surface water, and outside a floodplain.

Who do I call for a dead deer in the road? Your county public works department for a county road, or the Washington State Patrol or local police for a state highway — they'll notify WSDOT.

Is it dangerous to leave a dead deer on my property? It's a real, if usually low, risk — decomposition, odor, scavengers, and parasites that can affect dogs are all genuine concerns the longer it sits, which is part of why the state gives you a 72-hour disposal window rather than no deadline at all.
