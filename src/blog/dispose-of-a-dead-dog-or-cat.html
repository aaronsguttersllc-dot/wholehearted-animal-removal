How to Dispose of a Dead Dog or Cat in Washington

Losing a dog or cat at home is its own kind of hard, and it's a genuinely different situation than losing a horse or other livestock — legally and practically. I've written in detail about how strict Washington's rules are for burying a horse; the good news, if there is any in a moment like this, is that the rules for a dog or cat are real, but much simpler.

The Law Is Simpler Than You'd Think

The strict five-acre, 300-foot rule I write about for horses (WAC 16-25-025) is a livestock-specific rule — it legally doesn't apply to a dog or cat at all. The rule that actually governs a pet is Washington's general dead animal disposal code (WAC 246-203-121), and it's a lot more permissive: at least three feet of soil cover, at least 100 feet from any well, spring, or surface water, not in a floodplain or somewhere it could contaminate groundwater, and disposed of or covered within 72 hours. There's no acreage minimum and no property-line setback in that rule. That said, some cities, counties, or HOAs may add their own restrictions on top of state law, so it's worth a quick check with your local jurisdiction if you're not sure your situation qualifies.

If Your Pet Died at the Vet vs. at Home

If your pet was euthanized or died at a vet clinic, the clinic typically arranges cremation through a third-party pet crematory they already work with — very few clinics own that equipment themselves. If your pet died at home, that same option is usually still available, but you'll generally need to bring your pet in or arrange transport yourself; a vet showing up at your house to collect a body isn't the norm.

Cremation Options

Private cremation, where your pet is cremated individually and the ashes are returned to you, typically runs somewhere in the $100 to $450 range depending on your pet's size — a small cat tends to land on the lower end, a large dog on the higher end. Communal cremation, where multiple pets are cremated together and ashes aren't returned, usually costs 50 to 75 percent less, often somewhere in the $50 to $200 range. Neither number is exact — actual pricing varies by provider — but it gives you a real sense of the gap between the two options before you call around.

County and Landfill Options

  • In King County, a pet under 15 pounds can legally go in your regular household garbage; larger pets can go to a vet, animal shelter, pet cemetery, or Cedar Hills Regional Landfill with an approved waste clearance
  • Lewis County's Animal Shelter accepts pet remains for disposal for a small fee, though they don't offer pickup — worth a call to confirm current pricing
  • Thurston County residents can contact Joint Animal Services directly, since a specific published drop-off policy wasn't something I could find online
  • On-property burial remains a real, legal option for most pet owners under the state rule above, as long as your local jurisdiction doesn't add its own restriction

Pet Cemeteries

If you want something more permanent than cremation alone, real pet cemeteries operate in Washington, typically offering a full package — individual cremation, a cremains container, a headstone, and a burial plot — starting somewhere around $580 and increasing from there depending on your pet's size and what you choose.

Why Someone Calls a Removal Service Instead

None of the options above are wrong, and plenty of people handle this themselves without ever needing to call anyone. But there are real, common reasons people don't: renting rather than owning your home, which usually means you don't have the right to bury on the property; not having a vehicle you're willing or able to use to transport your pet; needing it handled the same day rather than whenever you can get an appointment; or simply not being able to face doing it yourself right after a loss. That last one isn't a small thing — grief over a pet is real grief, and there's nothing wrong with wanting help with the physical part of it so you can focus on the rest.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I legally bury my dog or cat in my backyard in Washington? In most cases, yes — the rule for pets is far simpler than the one for livestock, mainly requiring proper depth and distance from wells and water, with no acreage minimum. Check for any added local or HOA restrictions first.

How much does pet cremation cost in Washington? Roughly $100 to $450 for private cremation with ashes returned, or $50 to $200 for communal cremation without ashes returned, depending on your pet's size and the provider.

Will my vet come pick up my pet's body from my house? Usually not. Most vets arrange cremation through a third-party crematory rather than offering home pickup themselves.
