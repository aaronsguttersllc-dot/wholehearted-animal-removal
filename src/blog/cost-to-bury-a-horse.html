How Much Does It Cost to Bury a Horse in Washington?

I've written before about why most Washington properties don't actually qualify to legally bury a horse at all — the acreage and distance requirements rule out the majority of horse owners before cost even comes into it. But if your property genuinely does qualify, this is the question I get next: what does doing it right actually cost? Here's the honest answer, including the parts that don't show up in a simple price estimate.

What "Legal" Requires, Briefly

Washington's rule for burying livestock (WAC 16-25-025) requires at least five acres, a burial site at least 300 feet from any well, spring, or surface water, 300 feet from a neighbor's residence, and 50 feet from a property line, with at least three feet of soil covering the carcass. A single site is limited to one animal over 1,000 pounds. If any of that doesn't describe your property, the real options are private cremation or professional removal, not burial — I cover that in more detail in my post on what actually happens after a horse is euthanized.

The Real Equipment Cost

A hole big enough for a horse and deep enough to leave three feet of cover isn't something most people can dig by hand — this is backhoe or excavator work. National rental rates for a backhoe run roughly $250 to $1,000 or more per day depending on size and how long you need it, and a compact backhoe with delivery for a single day commonly lands around $700 to $1,100 once you factor in getting it to your property. If you're hiring a contractor with their own equipment rather than renting yourself, machine-and-operator rates in the Tacoma area run roughly $230 to $410 an hour, or $100 to $700 per cubic yard depending on soil conditions — those are general excavation cost-guide figures, not a livestock-specific rate, but they're a reasonable starting point for what you're actually looking at.

On sizing: the code only specifies three feet of cover, not exact hole dimensions, and I couldn't find an official source that publishes a standard size. Horse owners who've done this describe needing a hole roughly 6 to 8 feet deep and 7 to 8 feet long and wide to actually fit a horse and still leave proper cover once it settles — worth treating as a real-world reference point, not an official spec.

What This Actually Compares To

  • DIY burial: equipment rental cost above, plus your own time, physical risk, and no guarantee the job goes as planned on the first try
  • Private cremation: typically $600 to $2,000 for a horse-sized animal, with ashes returned
  • Rendering: rarely available at all, and especially unlikely if the animal was euthanized, since renderers generally won't accept a carcass with euthanasia drug residue
  • Professional removal and transport: pricing isn't something most companies publish, mine included, since it genuinely depends on location and access — worth an actual call for a real number rather than guessing

The Real Risk DIY Burial Doesn't Show You Up Front

A decomposing carcass isn't inert once it's in the ground. Full decomposition takes roughly two years, and during that time, leachate carrying real pathogens — Salmonella and E. coli among them — can move through soil toward groundwater, according to peer-reviewed research on carcass burial risk. Washington State University's own extension office specifically warns that some soils in this state have a water table just a few feet below the surface, which can make hitting the legal three-foot cover requirement while still keeping proper distance from groundwater genuinely difficult, not just a paperwork formality.

None of this means burial is a bad option where it's genuinely legal and the property is suited for it. It means the sticker price of renting a backhoe isn't the whole picture — site conditions matter as much as equipment cost.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to rent equipment to bury a horse? Roughly $250 to $1,000+ per day for a backhoe, or $700 to $1,100 for a single day with delivery — real ranges, not a fixed price, since it depends on equipment size and rental duration.

Do I need a permit to bury a horse on my property? A burial done within Washington's legal requirements generally doesn't require a separate landfill permit, but it does need to meet the real acreage and distance rules under WAC 16-25-025.

Is burial cheaper than professional removal? Not necessarily once you count equipment rental, your own time, and the physical and biosecurity risk of doing it yourself. It's worth getting a real removal quote before assuming DIY is the cheaper path.
